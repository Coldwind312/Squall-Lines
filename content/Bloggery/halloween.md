title: The Hallowe'en Argument
---
**31 October 2026**
**The day before the Kalends of November, 2779**

The argument's on again. It's a yearly event, a tradition as much as the pumpkins and spoopycreppy and all the dangly bats you could shake an exhumed tibia at.

Hallowe'en is approaching. And the social media channels, as always, light up with pagans complaining that Christians nicked their end-of-summer festival; and Christians complaining that the end-of-summer festival they *allegedly* nicked is too pagan and is placing your very soul—yes, *yours*—in jeopardy.

So let's get a couple of things squared away before we get properly started.

No, Christianity did not nick, yoink or otherwise purloin any ancient pre-Christian autumn festival—depending, of course, on your definition of these words. As with the similar argybargy scheduled for Easter and Christmas, it is a reality that Christianity fixed festival dates at times when older societies already had their own observances. And, to some degree, this might well have been a strategy to facilitate conversion: "Look, you can be One In Christ and not have to give up your traditional knees-ups[^1]".

Before you run away with that, though, and start swinging it round your head and yelling, you should consider that virtually every culture there's ever been—certainly at this kind of latitude—has had a pretty similar batch of festivals at the same times of year. Because we're all dealing with the cycle of seasons.

I mean not so much *now*, because we've utterly fucked that cycle up with all our carbon dioxide and microplastics and shit—but not so long ago, and certainly when most of these traditions originated, seasons were a thing that happened fairly reliably at fairly fixed intervals.

Most yearly festivals, whatever their specific religious or cultural significance, are where they are because that time of year is important. There's usually a spring festival, when life begins to return after the long, cold winter. There's a midsummer festival, when the sun is at its most powerful, the weather is glorious, and the planting has been done. There are harvest festivals marking the end of summer and the onset of autumn, when the crops are gathered in and the grain stores are filled ready for winter. And there's a midwinter festival, when the world is at its darkest, but you've mostly survived, and now there's promise of brightening days and slowly warming temperatures.

Now this isn't to say that these festivals can't mark other important events too. Easter, for Christians, celebrates the resurrection of Jesus after his crucifixion: it is a festival of new life. And perhaps it's for scholars and anthropologists to argue exactly how firmly that connection should be drawn. I'm not historian enough to be able to say "It's all just coincidence", and I'm not going to.

What I am going to say is that *for the purposes of those observances*, it doesn't really matter. Easter is the Christian celebration of Christ's return from the dead. That the approximate same time is celebrated by pagans as the spring festival of Ostara, and that the names and even the concepts may be related, and that, historically speaking, there may have been influence or derivation in one direction or another, does *not* make Easter a 'pagan festival' or 'stolen from pagans', or Ostara a Christian one or a 'demonic perversion' of a Christian one.

See, the true meaning of a festival is *what those observing that festival bring to it*. It—as is so commonly the case—depends on who's looking. So it is with Easter and Ostara; so it is with Yule and Christmas; and so it is with Hallowe'en and Samhain.

##### WORDS

I'm a sucker for words, me.

Hallowe'en, often written without the apostrophe but I'm being picky, is the common name for 'All Hallows' Eve'. 'Eve' is from 'even', related to 'evening' and 'eventide', and it was in older times written 'e'en', and pronounced 'een'. Hence we have 'Halloween'.

'Hallow' is from the Old English term 'halig', and it literally translates as 'holy': the word *holy* in modern English descends directly from *halig*. As a verb, to hallow something means to make it holy, or show it reverence as something sacred, which is the origin of the line in the Lord's Prayer, "Hallowed by thy name"—and somewhat less piously, the repeated phrase in the later seasons of *Stargate SG-1*, "Hallowed are the Ori".

A person who is considered inherently holy can also be referred to by the same word as a noun: they are 'a hallow'. And this term was formerly applied to saints in the western Catholic Church, which gives us the Feast of All Hallows—All Saints—which is celebrated by the Church on 01 November.

But Hallowe'en is just one date in what's known in Christianity—at least some branches thereof—as 'Allhallowstide'. This is actually three days of observance, and it begins with All Hallows' Eve(n)—Hallowe'en. This is traditionally a day of preparation, and opens Allhallowstide with a Vespers service in the evening of October 31. All Hallows follows, and commemorates saints and martyrs—those made holy by their devotion and often sacrifice in the name of their faith.

After that, on November 02, comes the Feast of All Souls. Not to be confused, as I long did: for the longest time I thought All Saints and All Souls were two terms for the same thing but they're not. All Souls is a broader remembrance of the dead.

Now here's one of those influences: the association of Hallowe'en with a thinning of the barrier between the living and the dead stems from older Celtic beliefs associated with Samhain, which held that the boundary between the real world and the otherworld blurred on this night.

But the general idea of both Allhallowtide and the Samhain observance is that the world is darkening, the land is huddling down ready for winter, and communities tend to reflect on endings—especially throughout most of history, where surviving through the winter was a much more uncertain prospect for many more people that it is today.

AND ROMANS, OBVIOUSLY

Yes, yes, there was going to be that bit. What's the equivalent of Hallowe'en in Roman tradition? After all, they saw dead people—so wouldn't they have had something similar?

And... yup. Kind of.
